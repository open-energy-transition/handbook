# AI good practices

This page outlines the good practices in AI applied by Open Energy Transition. 

## **1\. Purpose**

Artificial Intelligence (AI) creates opportunities for Open Energy Transition (OET), but also risks for people, knowledge, privacy, software, intellectual property, independence, costs, and the environment. To use AI tools effectively and efficiently within the OET, it is important to agree on and discuss these common good practices described in this document. It supports AI literacy, transparency, human oversight, data protection, and careful adoption of AI tools.

## **2\. Core principles**

OET uses AI as an assistance tool, not as a replacement for human responsibility.

AI use at OET should follow these principles:

* **A human remains responsible for the final output and needs to understand what has been created by the AI.**  
* **Material AI use should be disclosed.**  
* **Enforce Reuse-First Engineering.**  
* **Confidential, personal, partner, client, or non-public OET data must not be entered into security AI tools.**  
* **AI should help people learn and think, not replace their expertise.**  
* **OET should avoid unnecessary dependency on a single AI vendor.**  
* **AI should be used efficiently and with awareness of cost and environmental impact.**

## **3\. Be honest about AI usage**

Any document, image, code, dataset, presentation or other digital asset created with the support of AI must clearly indicate this when a contribution level of 0 or 1 is released to the public or shared with other colleagues. This can be done verbally or in the document itself when it is used internally. Don't pretend that you have reviewed AI-generated content when you haven't, and let others review it.

OET uses four AI contribution levels:

1. **🔴 Raw AI output**  
   AI-generated content that has not been meaningfully reviewed by a human. It must not be published or relied upon as an OET product; it is only permitted for prototyping/scoping purposes. Such developments need to be clearly labeled for the users as AI generated prototypes.   
2. **🟡 AI-generated and human-reviewed**  
   Content substantially generated by AI and then checked, corrected, and approved by a human. This is allowed for OET products, provided that the prompts are disclosed and the use of AI is accepted by the stakeholder, project manager or maintainer.    
3. **🔵 Human-generated and AI-assisted**  
   Content that is primarily created by humans and then improved using AI, for example through editing, providing feedback, translating text, suggesting code, or brainstorming. This is permitted for OET products, provided that the AI-generated content is significantly smaller compared to the human contribution.   
4. **🟢 Human-generated (default if nothing else is specified)**   
   Content created by a human without directly copying AI-generated text, code, images, or other substantive AI output. AI feedback or review is still allowed. Spelling checks and minor autocompletion using AI are still permitted when reviewed. 

## **4\. Responsible AI use practices**

### **4.1 You control the AI. You are the architect.** 

OET staff remain responsible for all content, code, decisions, and actions created through their accounts. AI must not be used to produce work / products that the responsible person cannot understand, verify, explain, or maintain when needed. For documents, the responsible person must check factual accuracy, sources, tone, confidentiality, and suitability for publication. The responsible developer must understand, review, test and maintain AI-generated code in core open-source software projects and client software. In particular, test cases should be fully controlled and thoroughly understood by the humans responsible for its development. Management staff at OET should clearly communicate how AI is allowed to be used for different purposes and products. This can be done via context files, which are provided to the AI prompt, similar to the [CLAUDE.md](http://CLAUDE.md) file designed for Stanford computer science students.   Key questions you should ask yourself always when using AI: 

1. Do I still learn about the subject while using AI?    
2. Does it free up mental resources that can be invested more effectively in another relevant area of your job, or are you simply outsourcing your intelligence without developing new skills? 

If you stop learning while using AI, you are using it in the wrong way. 

### **4.2 Limits on AI agents and automated actions**

Fully autonomous AI agents must not be used with OET accounts, systems, repositories, devices, or cloud infrastructure unless explicitly approved through a documented risk review. AI tools must not receive administrative permissions, production credentials, or authority to execute irreversible actions. Agents should not be run fully autonomously in normal user spaces, but rather on dedicated machines / user space. 

### **4.3 Do not use AI as your only interface to the world**

AI tools are not a substitute for research, human exchange, human domain knowledge, software engineering, or critical thinking. Use primary sources, conventional search engines, official documentation, scientific literature, peer review, and conversations with people. AI can be biased, outdated, incomplete, or overconfident. Important work should be checked against independent sources. Read critical original sources yourself. 

### **4.4 Protect private and non-public data**

Do not enter private or non-public data into unauthorised AI tools such as:

* personal data;  
* HR or recruitment information;  
* private correspondence;  
* partner, donor, client, or funder information;  
* unpublished research;  
* confidential strategy or internal planning;  
* proprietary datasets;  
* contracts, legal advice, or financial information;  
* passwords, credentials, API keys, tokens, or secrets;  
* private code or security-sensitive information.

### **4.5 Understand AI business models and avoid dependencies**

AI tools can create technical, intellectual, and commercial dependencies. Many AI providers currently offer services at prices that may not reflect the full long-term cost of operating their services. This can create supply chain and cost risks if workflows, software development, or knowledge work become difficult to maintain without continued access to specific AI tools and services. This becomes particularly problematic when AI-driven software infrastructure and products cannot be maintained anymore by humans when needed. 

As with other digital products, most AI services are designed to maximise user engagement, retention, and vendor lock-in. OET should therefore avoid building critical workflows around one proprietary provider, one model, or one account. Where practical, workflows should remain documented, portable, and maintainable without a specific AI assistant.

OET should avoid workflows that can only be maintained through one proprietary vendor, one model, or one account.

**4.6 Enforce Reuse-First Engineering**

The AI loves reinventing the wheel. Instead, tell it to reuse as many open-source projects as possible while minimising the amount of new code it creates. This could harm AI business models because AI does not generate lots of new code that ends up in your context windows, thereby repeatedly increasing costs. Ultimately, this ensures that the open-source approach remains the most effective. Always remember: Why Every Line is a Liability and should be considered technical debt. The best developer is the one who finds a solution without creating new code\! A Claude skill that is “Open Source Ecosystem” aware [could look like this](https://hackmd.io/@YhPkDVi5RJK7NY7ttj2HZQ/Sy8E9ENWGg).

### **4.7 Understand environmental impact and costs**

Use AI efficiently. Avoid unnecessary long prompts, repeated upload of the same large documents, and generation of unused outputs.

Good practices include:

* provide clear and relevant context;  
* reuse approved prompts and summaries;  
* share reusable AI-generated materials internally where appropriate;  
* choose smaller or local models where sufficient;  
* track significant AI-related costs and environmental impact.  
* inform yourself on the environmental impact.   
* clear your context window regularly

### **4.8 Build AI literacy and share good practices**

Anyone using AI for OET work should have a good understanding of the basic capabilities and limitations of their chosen tools. These include risks of hallucination, bias, data protection, confidentiality, licensing, security, environmental impact and the appropriate disclosure of AI use. OET staff are encouraged to share useful workflows, prompts, review methods and lessons learned with their colleagues. This includes company AI configuration files, such as a CLAUDE.md, for various use cases. Juniors are given the time and space to understand their domain field to become senior at one point, being able to control and be responsible for AI generated content.

### **4.9 Escalate sensitive or high-risk use cases**

Additional review is required before using AI for workflows involving:

* recruitment, hiring, promotion, or performance assessment;  
* decisions about individuals or organisations;  
* sensitive personal data;  
* public-facing tools that interact directly with people;  
* realistic generated or manipulated images, audio, or video;  
* cybersecurity, infrastructure, or administrative access;  
* work that could materially affect OET’s credibility or legal obligations.

### **4.10 Report AI-related incidents**

AI-related issues should be reported internally when they involve possible data disclosure, false public claims, security vulnerabilities, copyright concerns, harmful outputs, or misuse of AI tools.

The goal is learning and risk reduction, not blame.
